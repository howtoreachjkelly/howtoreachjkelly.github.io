01 — Snapshot

What Blueprint Biosecurity is

A nonprofit trying to make sure the next outbreak doesn't become the next pandemic — by building physical, deployable defenses, not by working on surveillance or AI policy.

Founded in 2023 by Jake Swett, Blueprint is a 501(c)(3) working on practical, pathogen-agnostic countermeasures: things that work regardless of what the next pathogen turns out to be. Swett started the organization directly out of his work shaping the Apollo Program for Biodefense, a post-COVID report for the Bipartisan Commission on Biodefense that laid out a broad plan for overhauling America's pandemic defenses. Blueprint exists to carry pieces of that agenda forward.

The organization's method is distinctive and worth understanding before the call: they pick a small number of underdeveloped but promising technologies, build the evidence base through funded research, and package findings into a "Blueprint" — a public roadmap aimed at researchers, funders, and policymakers. It's a translation function as much as a research function.

03 — Portfolio

The programs, corrected

Correction from the Lesley Marling call
The earlier version of this dossier treated PPE, Far-UVC, and BETS/Glycol as three parallel programs. That's wrong. Per Lesley: there are really two program-director lines — PPE, and Built Environment (BETS) — and Far-UVC sits inside BETS as a work stream alongside Glycols, not as its own parallel track. BETS/Built Environment is, in Lesley's words, "the largest and fastest growing team we have." Worth using the corrected structure on the call rather than the three-way framing from earlier prep.

PPE — reusable respirators for critical workers

The 2025 focus was validating assumptions about elastomeric half-mask respirator (EHMR) effectiveness through a hands-on engineering partnership with Amodo Design. Early findings were more behavioral than technical: without training, a meaningful share of users put the respirators on backwards; training affected pass/fail rates on fit tests more than the equipment itself; and a good fit test doesn't reliably predict a good fit later. Completed 100+ fit and user-experience tests. 2026 plans center on RFPs examining filtration efficiency loss and the needs of critical infrastructure workers.

Built Environment (BETS) — the larger program, with Far-UVC and Glycols nested inside it

This is Blueprint's largest and fastest-growing team. Far-UVC is its best-known and most externally visible work stream: 2025's central event was publishing the Blueprint for Far-UVC after nearly two years of literature review and expert interviews, plus 13 funded grants and 11 conference presentations. Glycol vapors is the newer, less mature work stream inside the same program — an older, under-studied airborne disinfection approach where 2025 was scoping and literature review, with the first grantee announcements landing in 2026 across five technical areas (triethylene, propylene, dipropylene glycol). The team's own honest self-assessment across both: adoption is constrained as much by cost, maintenance, regulatory uncertainty, and public perception as by whether the underlying technology works.

04 — How decisions actually get made

The strategic-to-operational flow, per Lesley

Directly confirmed, not previously documented anywhere public: Jake, Siobhan, the board, and funders make the large-scale strategic calls about where Blueprint invests — and in practice, that follows the money. Far-UVC and Built Environment work currently have the most funding, so that's where the most strategic energy goes; there's also funding earmarked for exploratory projects not yet public. 08 — The flagship publication, from the actual exec summary

The Far-UVC Blueprint

Updated against the real executive summary and recommendations document — the earlier version of this section undersold both the risk case and the recommendation structure.

Vacuum UV Far-UVC (200–230nm) Conventional GUV (254nm) UV-A Visible

Far-UVC is a shorter-wavelength form of germicidal ultraviolet light. Because it's absorbed almost entirely by the outer, non-living layer of skin and eyes, it doesn't penetrate to living tissue the way conventional germicidal UV does — which is why it can, in principle, run continuously in occupied rooms rather than only when spaces are empty. Nearly two years of literature synthesis plus over 100 expert interviews across photobiology, atmospheric chemistry, indoor air quality, building science, and public health went into the report. Lead author is Senior Fellow Richard Williamson.

Why they're framing this as urgent

The report opens with a fairly stark risk case, not just a technology pitch. COVID-19 is cited at roughly 27 million deaths; tuberculosis kills 1.6 million people a year; roughly a billion people are infected with influenza annually. On top of that baseline, the report names two categories of drivers pushing pandemic risk in the wrong direction: climate change, factory farming, and habitat encroachment more than doubling the odds of a pathogen jumping from animals to humans — and, stated directly and without hedging, rapid advances in AI and synthetic biology increasing vulnerability to malicious actors and engineered pandemics. The report cites expert estimates of roughly a 1-in-8 chance of a pandemic killing over 90 million people by 2050 — three times COVID's toll.

Relevant to the positioning question
This isn't a soft, secondary gesture toward AI risk — it's in the second paragraph of the executive summary, stated as one of two named drivers of rising pandemic risk. It's still not the organization's operating focus day to day (their actual work is photobiology and air chemistry, not AI), but it's a more direct and more central connection than the earlier version of this dossier gave it credit for. Worth recalibrating how you'd answer "why biosecurity, given your AI safety background" accordingly — you have more to point to here than a stretch.

Where far-UVC fits against existing tools

The report is explicit that far-UVC isn't meant to replace vaccines, PPE, or ventilation — each has real limitations it's positioned to address. Vaccines take too long to develop and distribute for a fast-moving outbreak. PPE depends on individual compliance, which erodes over time. Ventilation and filtration are proven but often can't hit the clean-air targets the ASHRAE 241 standard calls for without disruptive noise, drafts, or energy cost — the report gives a concrete example: a restaurant would need roughly 60 additional cubic feet per minute of clean airflow per person to meet that standard, comparable to an operating theater. Conventional upper-room UV works but doesn't scale well, since it requires expert installation and keeping people out of the direct beam. Far-UVC's pitch is that it can hit those clean-air targets passively, without depending on anyone's behavior.

The actual recommendation structure — 12 recommendations, three tiers

The earlier version of this dossier only surfaced 4 recommendations. There are 12, organized in three tiers by urgency:

Tier 1 — Four urgent research priorities

  • Establish how installations need to be designed to actually suppress transmission (dose-response by pathogen, room geometry modeling, environmental variables like humidity).
  • Identify how far-UVC's biological effects differ mechanistically from solar UV and conventional GUV, to properly ground safe exposure limits.
  • Understand and mitigate unintended air quality effects — ozone generation is the central concern.
  • Obtain high-quality real-world effectiveness evidence — ideally cluster-randomized trials, though the report notes ventilation itself became standard practice without one.

Tier 2 — Five recommendations for facilitating adoption at scale

  • Create simple, research-backed deployment guidance that public health agencies and trusted institutions can actually communicate.
  • Improve consensus standards (ASHRAE 241, several UL and IEC standards) — several are outdated or weren't written with far-UVC's properties in mind.
  • Ensure installations follow standards as part of a layered approach alongside ventilation and filtration, not as a replacement for them.
  • Improve performance and affordability of emitters — krypton chloride excimer lamps dominate today; cost per mW is the key metric, and scale alone should drive it down.
  • Create cost-benefit analysis frameworks decision-makers can actually use, starting with high-risk settings like healthcare and schools.

Tier 3 — Three longer-term research agenda items

  • Long-term safety studies in diverse populations under chronic exposure.
  • Study far-UVC's effects on common building materials, cosmetics, clothing, and plants.
  • Build deeper mechanistic understanding of how far-UVC actually inactivates pathogens — useful groundwork for future biosensors.

One nuance worth being able to speak to: the report's own scope note says it covers only long-range airborne transmission suppression — near-field protection, surface disinfection, and short-range transmission are flagged as promising but out of scope for this version, with a future update expected to cover them.

09 — Your own read

Notes and questions worth bringing

Working through the actual executive summary raised sharper questions than the summary alone did. Condensed here so they're usable on the call, not just in your head.

The trust/consent trade-off — fluoridation is the closer analogy, not vaccines

Vaccines still require individual consent and administration. Water fluoridation is the tighter parallel: continuous, passive, population-wide exposure delivered through a trusted public utility, where opting out means moving or plumbing a separate source. It's also a live cautionary tale right now, not just a historical one — several U.S. cities and states have voted to remove fluoridation in 2024–2025 in the same climate of science skepticism the report itself is worried about, and that happened after 70+ years of safety data. That's a sharper warning than seatbelt laws or smoking bans, which regulate behavior rather than exposure.

On whether the report downplays risk — the opposite problem is more accurate

The language isn't spin. It's dense academic hedging — "not yet fully understood," "effects not traditionally associated with." That's honest, but it's not risk communication, it's a kind of risk avoidance: careful enough that no claim can be pinned down as reassuring or alarming. Fine for an expert or funder audience. Not a model of the lay-audience communication the report itself says still needs to be built — this document is the input to that process (see Recommendation 5), not an example of it. Worth noting the org seems to already know this: building that communication capability is what Government Affairs and Communications exist to do.

Precedent on UV biology — strong in general, thin for this specific band

Solar UV and conventional 254nm GUV have decades of occupational and epidemiological data — that's exactly why ICNIRP and ACGIH exposure limits exist, and why the report can say "no evidence of significant harm" with confidence. Recommendation 2's gap is narrower and legitimate: far-UVC's mechanism (near-total absorption in the outermost dead cell layer) differs enough from those better-studied wavelengths that the existing action spectra may not transfer cleanly to the 200–230nm band specifically.

Ozone mitigation as an actual product — a real white space

The report calls for industry to develop mitigation technology but never proposes a paired consumer or commercial product. Activated-carbon-plus-UV combinations already exist in aircraft cabin air systems and some commercial scrubbers — the underlying tech isn't novel. What's missing is someone packaging it specifically as the standard companion product to a far-UVC install.

Product designers are missing from the stakeholder list — and it's telling

Recommendation 5.1's list (modelers, experimentalists, public health authorities, comms experts, practitioners) is a science-and-policy list. No one on it is thinking about industrial design, UX, or how a facilities manager wants the thing to look and mount. Real blind spot for a science-heavy team — and arguably the kind of gap an operations-minded Managing Director is positioned to name that the current team wouldn't think to.

Cintas, not Apple, is the realistic near-term commercial play

Emitters are still expensive, procurement is still mostly institutional, and regulatory status is unsettled — a hard environment for consumer electronics. Cintas-style B2B is the better near-term fit: they already sell recurring safety compliance as a service (mats, first aid kits, uniforms) to exactly the facilities managers who'd buy this. An Apple-style consumer device is a more plausible 5–10 year vision than a near-term opportunity, per the report's own emitter-cost timeline.

Marketing should follow the science, and the report's caution is a deliberate reaction, not institutional timidity

FTC/FDA rules generally require evidence before health claims. COVID produced plenty of counter-examples where narrative outran evidence — ionizers, unproven "UV wands," hydroxychloroquine hype — and did real damage to public trust in the category. That history is almost certainly why this report over-hedges rather than under-hedges. Better marketing of good science is a genuinely useful idea in the abstract; it's a dangerous one to get backwards in this specific field.

6.1 vs. 6.2 standards overlap — mostly not covered, despite how it reads

6.1's list (ASHRAE 241, UL 8802/8803, IEC 62471, ISO 15858, RP 27.1) contains no ozone-testing methodology built for far-UVC's specific spectral profile — that gap in 6.2 is genuine new work, not a restatement. The device-labeling standard in 6.2 overlaps more, since IEC 62471 and RP 27.1 already govern photobiological safety disclosure, but neither mandates the consumer-facing lifetime/power/spectrum labeling 6.2 asks for.

"Don't these markets exist already after COVID?" — partially, but not for far-UVC specifically

Conventional GUV markets in water treatment, surface disinfection, and food processing are genuinely mature. COVID did produce a UV-branded consumer boom, but a lot of it was unregulated, non-far-UVC "UV-C wand" marketing that regulators and researchers have since had to walk back. Far-UVC's specific niche — safe-by-design, continuous, occupied-space use — doesn't yet have that market penetration, which is why the report labels these uses "proposed" rather than evaluated.

Cost-benefit studies as a marketing opportunity — already their playbook, just not narrated that way

The PPE program's engineering studies with Amodo Design directly became the evidence base behind the appropriations wins. Framing future far-UVC cost-benefit work the same way — evidence generation that doubles as the policy and funding pitch — isn't a new idea for them, it's a continuation of what's already working.

Short-term vs. long-term deployment — the logic exists, just isn't named as a doctrine

Recommendation 10 already makes close to this point: requiring long-term safety data before deploying an efficacious, life-saving intervention would be "an excessive application of the precautionary principle." That's the crisis-deployment override, stated but not formalized into a framework. Proposing an explicit two-tier structure — looser evidence standards for emergency deployment, a higher bar for routine or steady-state use — is a genuinely useful structuring idea to bring to the conversation, not something you'd be repeating back to them.

Your strongest fit argument — stronger than the AI bridge

Built environment is where your actual experience sits

Hatch built small enclosed physical environments for public occupancy. Yahoo/RYOT ran occupied studio production environments specifically during the pandemic, under exactly the kind of built-environment safety constraints this program studies. Cassette operates under active CA HCD regulatory compliance for occupied structures. None of that needs reframing or stretching to fit — it's direct, documented, operational experience with the built-environment side of what Blueprint studies. It doesn't require the AI-risk bridge to justify the conversation, and it's worth leading with on the call, not holding in reserve behind the AI framing.
