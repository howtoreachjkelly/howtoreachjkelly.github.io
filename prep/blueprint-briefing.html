01 — Snapshot

What Blueprint Biosecurity is

A nonprofit trying to make sure the next outbreak doesn't become the next pandemic — by building physical, deployable defenses, not by working on surveillance or AI policy.

Founded in 2023 by Jake Swett, Blueprint is a 501(c)(3) working on practical, pathogen-agnostic countermeasures: things that work regardless of what the next pathogen turns out to be. Swett started the organization directly out of his work shaping the Apollo Program for Biodefense, a post-COVID report for the Bipartisan Commission on Biodefense that laid out a broad plan for overhauling America's pandemic defenses. Blueprint exists to carry pieces of that agenda forward.

The organization's method is distinctive and worth understanding before the call: they pick a small number of underdeveloped but promising technologies, build the evidence base through funded research, and package findings into a "Blueprint" — a public roadmap aimed at researchers, funders, and policymakers. It's a translation function as much as a research function.

03 — Portfolio

The programs, corrected

Correction from the Lesley Marling call
The earlier version of this dossier treated PPE, Far-UVC, and BETS/Glycol as three parallel programs. That's wrong. Per Lesley: there are really two program-director lines — PPE, and Built Environment (BETS) — and Far-UVC sits inside BETS as a work stream alongside Glycols, not as its own parallel track. BETS/Built Environment is, in Lesley's words, "the largest and fastest growing team we have." Worth using the corrected structure on the call rather than the three-way framing from earlier prep.

PPE — reusable respirators for critical workers

The 2025 focus was validating assumptions about elastomeric half-mask respirator (EHMR) effectiveness through a hands-on engineering partnership with Amodo Design. Early findings were more behavioral than technical: without training, a meaningful share of users put the respirators on backwards; training affected pass/fail rates on fit tests more than the equipment itself; and a good fit test doesn't reliably predict a good fit later. Completed 100+ fit and user-experience tests. 2026 plans center on RFPs examining filtration efficiency loss and the needs of critical infrastructure workers.

Built Environment (BETS) — the larger program, with Far-UVC and Glycols nested inside it

This is Blueprint's largest and fastest-growing team. Far-UVC is its best-known and most externally visible work stream: 2025's central event was publishing the Blueprint for Far-UVC after nearly two years of literature review and expert interviews, plus 13 funded grants and 11 conference presentations. Glycol vapors is the newer, less mature work stream inside the same program — an older, under-studied airborne disinfection approach where 2025 was scoping and literature review, with the first grantee announcements landing in 2026 across five technical areas (triethylene, propylene, dipropylene glycol). The team's own honest self-assessment across both: adoption is constrained as much by cost, maintenance, regulatory uncertainty, and public perception as by whether the underlying technology works.

The tradeoffs of spinning GA into a separate C4

Siobhan raised this herself as something she's actively weighing — worth having a real, worked-through view rather than a surface reaction if it comes up.

Why it genuinely helps: a 501(c)(3) is legally capped on lobbying spend — even electing the more generous 501(h) expenditure test still limits it, with a tighter sub-cap on "grassroots" lobbying specifically. A 501(c)(4) can make lobbying its primary activity with no dollar ceiling, and can engage in partisan political activity as long as that isn't its *primary* purpose, which a C3 can never do at all. Most concretely — and the one Siobhan named directly — a C4 can establish and fund a connected PAC in a way a C3 structurally cannot. Separation also gives the technical/research side a cleaner firewall: funders, academic partners, and researchers may not want to be seen as adjacent to lobbying or PAC activity, and a hard legal separation makes "the science is nonpartisan, the advocacy arm is separate" a much easier claim to defend.

What it actually costs: donations to a C4 aren't tax-deductible — a real fundraising lift, since much of Blueprint's current donor base likely gives because it is. It also means duplicate legal and administrative infrastructure: separate governance, filings, likely state lobbying registrations, and a cost-allocation agreement between the two entities, since the IRS doesn't allow the C3 to subsidize the C4's lobbying work — exactly the kind of cross-entity accounting complexity that's hard to get right at a 20-person org. GA currently operates as "leased employees" embedded informally at the C3; a genuine spinoff, especially with its own VP of Ops and MD as Siobhan floated, means running two organizations instead of one, at the exact moment this Managing Director role exists because internal coordination is already straining. There's also a real risk to the technical-to-narrative translation pipeline (see the Questions section) — a more siloed GA could make that handoff harder to manage, not easier, unless the bridge between the two entities is deliberately designed rather than assumed. And more aggressive lobbying and any PAC activity raises real reputational exposure for an org that brands itself as nonpartisan and evidence-based, if the C4's more political posture isn't kept visibly separate from the C3's scientific credibility.

One-line version if it comes up: a C4 buys real advocacy capacity Blueprint doesn't have today, at the cost of donor economics, administrative overhead, and — ironically — a fresh coordination problem layered on top of the one this role already exists to solve.

08 — The flagship publication, from the actual exec summary

The Far-UVC Blueprint

Updated against the real executive summary and recommendations document — the earlier version of this section undersold both the risk case and the recommendation structure.

Vacuum UV Far-UVC (200–230nm) Conventional GUV (254nm) UV-A Visible

Far-UVC is a shorter-wavelength form of germicidal ultraviolet light. Because it's absorbed almost entirely by the outer, non-living layer of skin and eyes, it doesn't penetrate to living tissue the way conventional germicidal UV does — which is why it can, in principle, run continuously in occupied rooms rather than only when spaces are empty. Nearly two years of literature synthesis plus over 100 expert interviews across photobiology, atmospheric chemistry, indoor air quality, building science, and public health went into the report. Lead author is Senior Fellow Richard Williamson.

Why they're framing this as urgent

The report opens with a fairly stark risk case, not just a technology pitch. COVID-19 is cited at roughly 27 million deaths; tuberculosis kills 1.6 million people a year; roughly a billion people are infected with influenza annually. On top of that baseline, the report names two categories of drivers pushing pandemic risk in the wrong direction: climate change, factory farming, and habitat encroachment more than doubling the odds of a pathogen jumping from animals to humans — and, stated directly and without hedging, rapid advances in AI and synthetic biology increasing vulnerability to malicious actors and engineered pandemics. The report cites expert estimates of roughly a 1-in-8 chance of a pandemic killing over 90 million people by 2050 — three times COVID's toll.

Relevant to the positioning question
This isn't a soft, secondary gesture toward AI risk — it's in the second paragraph of the executive summary, stated as one of two named drivers of rising pandemic risk. It's still not the organization's operating focus day to day (their actual work is photobiology and air chemistry, not AI), but it's a more direct and more central connection than the earlier version of this dossier gave it credit for. Worth recalibrating how you'd answer "why biosecurity, given your AI safety background" accordingly — you have more to point to here than a stretch.

Where far-UVC fits against existing tools

The report is explicit that far-UVC isn't meant to replace vaccines, PPE, or ventilation — each has real limitations it's positioned to address. Vaccines take too long to develop and distribute for a fast-moving outbreak. PPE depends on individual compliance, which erodes over time. Ventilation and filtration are proven but often can't hit the clean-air targets the ASHRAE 241 standard calls for without disruptive noise, drafts, or energy cost — the report gives a concrete example: a restaurant would need roughly 60 additional cubic feet per minute of clean airflow per person to meet that standard, comparable to an operating theater. Conventional upper-room UV works but doesn't scale well, since it requires expert installation and keeping people out of the direct beam. Far-UVC's pitch is that it can hit those clean-air targets passively, without depending on anyone's behavior.

The actual recommendation structure — 12 recommendations, three tiers

The earlier version of this dossier only surfaced 4 recommendations. There are 12, organized in three tiers by urgency:

Tier 1 — Four urgent research priorities

  • Establish how installations need to be designed to actually suppress transmission (dose-response by pathogen, room geometry modeling, environmental variables like humidity).
  • Identify how far-UVC's biological effects differ mechanistically from solar UV and conventional GUV, to properly ground safe exposure limits.
  • Understand and mitigate unintended air quality effects — ozone generation is the central concern.
  • Obtain high-quality real-world effectiveness evidence — ideally cluster-randomized trials, though the report notes ventilation itself became standard practice without one.

Tier 2 — Five recommendations for facilitating adoption at scale

  • Create simple, research-backed deployment guidance that public health agencies and trusted institutions can actually communicate.
  • Improve consensus standards (ASHRAE 241, several UL and IEC standards) — several are outdated or weren't written with far-UVC's properties in mind.
  • Ensure installations follow standards as part of a layered approach alongside ventilation and filtration, not as a replacement for them.
  • Improve performance and affordability of emitters — krypton chloride excimer lamps dominate today; cost per mW is the key metric, and scale alone should drive it down.
  • Create cost-benefit analysis frameworks decision-makers can actually use, starting with high-risk settings like healthcare and schools.

Tier 3 — Three longer-term research agenda items

  • Long-term safety studies in diverse populations under chronic exposure.
  • Study far-UVC's effects on common building materials, cosmetics, clothing, and plants.
  • Build deeper mechanistic understanding of how far-UVC actually inactivates pathogens — useful groundwork for future biosensors.

One nuance worth being able to speak to: the report's own scope note says it covers only long-range airborne transmission suppression — near-field protection, surface disinfection, and short-range transmission are flagged as promising but out of scope for this version, with a future update expected to cover them.

09 — Your own read & questions

Notes and Questions

Working through the actual executive summary raised sharper questions than the summary alone did. Condensed here so they're usable on the call, not just in your head — followed by every question worth asking, grouped and sequenced.

The positioning question you should resolve first
Everything you've built so far — the website, the coursework, the application materials — is framed around AI safety and governance specifically. This is a biosecurity organization, and their day-to-day work (photobiology, air chemistry, respirator fit testing) has nothing to do with AI. Two arguments are available to you, and they're not equally strong. The AI-risk bridge is real — their own executive summary names AI advances as an explicit driver of pandemic risk — but it's a rationale you'd be constructing about their mission, not evidence about you. The built-environment argument (see below) is the stronger one: direct, documented operational experience with occupied physical spaces under safety constraints, across Hatch, RYOT, and Cassette, that doesn't need reframing to be relevant. Lead with that. Keep the AI bridge in reserve as a second, supporting thread — not the headline.

The trust/consent trade-off — fluoridation is the closer analogy, not vaccines

Vaccines still require individual consent and administration. Water fluoridation is the tighter parallel: continuous, passive, population-wide exposure delivered through a trusted public utility, where opting out means moving or plumbing a separate source. It's also a live cautionary tale right now, not just a historical one — several U.S. cities and states have voted to remove fluoridation in 2024–2025 in the same climate of science skepticism the report itself is worried about, and that happened after 70+ years of safety data. That's a sharper warning than seatbelt laws or smoking bans, which regulate behavior rather than exposure.

On whether the report downplays risk — the opposite problem is more accurate

The language isn't spin. It's dense academic hedging — "not yet fully understood," "effects not traditionally associated with." That's honest, but it's not risk communication, it's a kind of risk avoidance: careful enough that no claim can be pinned down as reassuring or alarming. Fine for an expert or funder audience. Not a model of the lay-audience communication the report itself says still needs to be built — this document is the input to that process (see Recommendation 5), not an example of it. Worth noting the org seems to already know this: building that communication capability is what Government Affairs and Communications exist to do.

Precedent on UV biology — strong in general, thin for this specific band

Solar UV and conventional 254nm GUV have decades of occupational and epidemiological data — that's exactly why ICNIRP and ACGIH exposure limits exist, and why the report can say "no evidence of significant harm" with confidence. Recommendation 2's gap is narrower and legitimate: far-UVC's mechanism (near-total absorption in the outermost dead cell layer) differs enough from those better-studied wavelengths that the existing action spectra may not transfer cleanly to the 200–230nm band specifically.

Ozone mitigation as an actual product — a real white space

The report calls for industry to develop mitigation technology but never proposes a paired consumer or commercial product. Activated-carbon-plus-UV combinations already exist in aircraft cabin air systems and some commercial scrubbers — the underlying tech isn't novel. What's missing is someone packaging it specifically as the standard companion product to a far-UVC install.

Product designers are missing from the stakeholder list — and it's telling

Recommendation 5.1's list (modelers, experimentalists, public health authorities, comms experts, practitioners) is a science-and-policy list. No one on it is thinking about industrial design, UX, or how a facilities manager wants the thing to look and mount. How layers get added, in practice: as a team's goals and domain genuinely expand, work naturally bifurcates into distinct streams — pods form around real seams in the work. Layers and management roles should emerge from that bifurcation, not precede it.

Who becomes a manager: not everyone, and not by default. Being an excellent individual contributor is a different skill from being an excellent manager, and the two don't automatically transfer. This is a point you made to Lesley — and one she independently arrived at and echoed back in her own words during the call, not just something you asserted and she absorbed. Worth telling Siobhan it's a shared view, not just your own.

What a layer is actually for, once added: not a control structure. A layer's job is to keep information flowing cleanly and keep lanes and goals clear for the people under it. Success as a manager is measured by whether the people below them feel unlocked and unblocked — not by how much oversight is being exercised. A manager doesn't need to master the domain of the team they run; they need to know enough to be "dangerous" — the language and the business goals — while trusting domain depth to the people doing the work.

Evidence this isn't theoretical: built a 10-person, two-layer team out of the AOL/Yahoo merger; later ran Head of Studios at ~250 people, four layers, 7–8 direct reports across four geographies. Also been through rapid downscaling, not just scaling — the "start flatter" instinct comes from having seen structure outlast its own justification, not only from having built structure once.

Applied to Blueprint specifically: at ~20 people with 3–4 layers already, and a stated plan to reach ~40 in a year and ~80 in 2–3, the live question isn't whether to add a management layer — that's already been decided by creating this role — it's whether the next layers after this one get added because the domain and goals have genuinely expanded, or because headcount growth alone creates pressure to add structure. Worth stating that distinction directly to Siobhan; it's a real point of view, not a comfortable platitude about flat organizations.

Before the call: fill in your own specifics

The pillars above are built from what's already documented. What's missing is the texture only you have — the actual stories. Worth having a real, specific answer ready for each of these before you're asked:

  • A specific time you coached someone technical or creative into becoming a better people manager themselves — not just managed them, but grew their management ability.
  • A specific time two strong, opinionated functional leads were in real conflict, and how you actually resolved it — not the version where everyone was reasonable and it worked out easily.
  • Your actual operating rhythm as a manager: 1:1 cadence, how you set and track goals, how you give hard feedback. Not a philosophy — the literal mechanics of what you do.
  • A time you had to hold a high bar and protect your team's autonomy at the same time — where those two things were in tension, not in harmony.
  • What you'd genuinely find hardest about managing directors who know more about their domain (photobiology, respirator engineering, federal policy) than you ever will.

These aren't rhetorical — write out real answers to each one before Tuesday. A confident, specific answer to "tell me about a time you coached a technical expert into being a better manager" is worth more in this conversation than any amount of polished framing about the three pillars above.

14 — Appendix B

Combined narrative: shared services and the human transition that followed

Story 2 (Studios → shared services) and the toxic-manager team-through-change story, sequenced as one continuous arc — the business model evolution first, then the human dimension that followed it.

Setup
  • Took over Studios during the Apollo-led take-private and a company-wide verticalization mandate — leadership change plus structural upheaval at the same time.
  • Studios ran as a shared central resource across four facilities (NY, LA, Bay Area, London), but cost allocation was headcount-based, disconnected from actual usage. Finance had far larger headcount than Sports and was billed accordingly regardless of which group actually used the studios.
  • A BU's bill reflected its size, not its usage — real dysfunction, hard to defend at budget time.
The escalation
  • Head of the Finance content vertical (senior to you) pushed back hardest and most publicly: ignored scheduling conventions, threatened to stop paying into maintenance costs, was openly vocal in a way that caused derision and instability across Studios.
  • Stated complaint was cost. Real motive was strategic: verticalizing his org as cleanly as possible strengthened his case for an eventual spinoff under Apollo.
The structural fix
  • Recognized this wouldn't resolve on its own and would get worse — restructured Studios into a genuine shared-services model as part of taking over.
  • Shrank the core org to its fixed-cost essentials only: facilities management, infrastructure, engineering, core operators.
  • Made everything else variable and directly attributable — project costs (contract labor, floating FTEs, production costs) charged back to whichever BU requested the work.
  • Sustained, consistent demand for a role converted into a BU-owned headcount request instead of hidden shared cost.
  • Approached the escalating Finance leader directly with the plan, despite him outranking you, and built buy-in from other business leaders before finalizing it.
Outcome (structural)
  • He agreed. Instability stopped. His org grew and later partnered with the central organization to build a dedicated facility for finance content.
  • Fixed and variable costs cleanly separated; capex became demand-driven instead of Studios-driven.
The human consequence
  • The other side of the same restructuring: several people on your team had to move out of Studios into other BU orgs as part of the breakup. The real alternative on the table was severance instead of the move.
  • One landed under a manager known for being critical, aggressive, and dismissive of healthy team culture — a reputation for disparaging people behind their back. Real fear and frustration, not hypothetical.
  • You had a repeated, honest conversation with each person facing the choice — naming the fear directly, then reframing toward what was in their control.
  • Followed through for six months after the moves, not just at the decision point.
Outcome (human), honestly mixed
  • All of them chose the move over severance.
  • Some flourished in their new teams.
  • Some struggled — but those tended to be people who already had a pattern of clashing with leadership, now in a more toxic environment that magnified an existing dynamic, not something your framing created or failed to prevent.
  • The specific person in the story adapted, stayed with the company, and ultimately outlasted the toxic manager.
The throughline to name explicitly
  • One event, two disciplines: solve the structural/economic problem with a repeatable mechanism (shared-services redesign), then solve the human fallout of that same structural decision with direct, honest, sustained engagement — not a program, a person-by-person conversation held to follow-through.
  • What you'd change: only the timing on the structural side — you'd recognize the pattern before it escalated to crisis next time. You wouldn't change the mechanism, and you wouldn't change how you handled the human side.
