01 — Snapshot

What Blueprint Biosecurity is

A nonprofit trying to make sure the next outbreak doesn't become the next pandemic — by building physical, deployable defenses, not by working on surveillance or AI policy.

Founded in 2023 by Jake Swett, Blueprint is a 501(c)(3) working on practical, pathogen-agnostic countermeasures: things that work regardless of what the next pathogen turns out to be. Swett started the organization directly out of his work shaping the Apollo Program for Biodefense, a post-COVID report for the Bipartisan Commission on Biodefense that laid out a broad plan for overhauling America's pandemic defenses. Blueprint exists to carry pieces of that agenda forward.

The organization's method is distinctive and worth understanding before the call: they pick a small number of underdeveloped but promising technologies, build the evidence base through funded research, and package findings into a "Blueprint" — a public roadmap aimed at researchers, funders, and policymakers. It's a translation function as much as a research function.

03 — Portfolio

The three programs, condensed

PPE — reusable respirators for critical workers

The 2025 focus was validating assumptions about elastomeric half-mask respirator (EHMR) effectiveness through a hands-on engineering partnership with Amodo Design. Early findings were more behavioral than technical: without training, a meaningful share of users put the respirators on backwards; training affected pass/fail rates on fit tests more than the equipment itself; and a good fit test doesn't reliably predict a good fit later. Completed 100+ fit and user-experience tests. 2026 plans center on RFPs examining filtration efficiency loss and the needs of critical infrastructure workers.

Far-UVC — continuous air disinfection

This is Blueprint's flagship and best-known program. 2025's central event was publishing the Blueprint for Far-UVC after nearly two years of literature review and expert interviews — a shift from "synthesizing the research" to "actively building the field" through 13 funded grants and 11 conference presentations. The team's own framing of where things stand: efficacy in controlled settings is now well-supported, but the open question has moved from "does it work" to "can lab results translate into deployment decision-makers actually trust during a crisis." 2026 work centers on early results from named studies (EXHALE, DEMIST, and an AIR Facility tuberculosis-transmission study in South Africa) feeding into a clinical trial protocol.

Built Environment Transmission Suppression (BETS) — the newer, broader bucket

A 2025 addition covering portable air cleaners, upper-room germicidal UV, and — as the most novel piece — glycol vapors, an older and under-studied airborne disinfection approach. The team's honest self-assessment: adoption of even the established tools (air cleaners, upper-room GUV) is constrained more by cost, maintenance, and low public awareness than by whether the technology works, and regulatory uncertainty and public perception matter as much as technical performance across the board. 2026 will bring grantee announcements across five technical areas of glycol vapor research (triethylene, propylene, dipropylene glycol).

Source: Blueprint Biosecurity 2025 Annual Report, Program Highlights sections

07 — Your own read, and how others see them

Notes for the call, plus outside perspective

Working through the actual executive summary raised sharper questions than the summary alone did. Condensed here so they're usable on the call, not just in your head — followed by how two outside sources place the organization. The full Far-UVC Blueprint summary — the risk case, the cost-effectiveness figures, and all 12 recommendations — now lives in a separate reference document rather than in this dossier; open it alongside these notes if you want the source detail behind what's discussed below.

The trust/consent trade-off — fluoridation is the closer analogy, not vaccines

Vaccines still require individual consent and administration. Water fluoridation is the tighter parallel: continuous, passive, population-wide exposure delivered through a trusted public utility, where opting out means moving or plumbing a separate source. It's also a live cautionary tale right now, not just a historical one — several U.S. cities and states have voted to remove fluoridation in 2024–2025 in the same climate of science skepticism the report itself is worried about, and that happened after 70+ years of safety data. That's a sharper warning than seatbelt laws or smoking bans, which regulate behavior rather than exposure.

On whether the report downplays risk — the opposite problem is more accurate

The language isn't spin. It's dense academic hedging — "not yet fully understood," "effects not traditionally associated with." That's honest, but it's not risk communication, it's a kind of risk avoidance: careful enough that no claim can be pinned down as reassuring or alarming. Fine for an expert or funder audience. Not a model of the lay-audience communication the report itself says still needs to be built — this document is the input to that process (see Recommendation 5), not an example of it. Worth noting the org seems to already know this: building that communication capability is what Government Affairs and Communications exist to do.

Precedent on UV biology — strong in general, thin for this specific band

Solar UV and conventional 254nm GUV have decades of occupational and epidemiological data — that's exactly why ICNIRP and ACGIH exposure limits exist, and why the report can say "no evidence of significant harm" with confidence. Recommendation 2's gap is narrower and legitimate: far-UVC's mechanism (near-total absorption in the outermost dead cell layer) differs enough from those better-studied wavelengths that the existing action spectra may not transfer cleanly to the 200–230nm band specifically.

Ozone mitigation as an actual product — a real white space

The report calls for industry to develop mitigation technology but never proposes a paired consumer or commercial product. Activated-carbon-plus-UV combinations already exist in aircraft cabin air systems and some commercial scrubbers — the underlying tech isn't novel. What's missing is someone packaging it specifically as the standard companion product to a far-UVC install.

Product designers are missing from the stakeholder list — and it's telling

Recommendation 5.1's list (modelers, experimentalists, public health authorities, comms experts, practitioners) is a science-and-policy list. No one on it is thinking about industrial design, UX, or how a facilities manager wants the thing to look and mount. Real blind spot for a science-heavy team — and arguably the kind of gap an operations-minded Managing Director is positioned to name that the current team wouldn't think to.

Cintas, not Apple, is the realistic near-term commercial play

Emitters are still expensive, procurement is still mostly institutional, and regulatory status is unsettled — a hard environment for consumer electronics. Cintas-style B2B is the better near-term fit: they already sell recurring safety compliance as a service (mats, first aid kits, uniforms) to exactly the facilities managers who'd buy this. An Apple-style consumer device is a more plausible 5–10 year vision than a near-term opportunity, per the report's own emitter-cost timeline.

Marketing should follow the science, and the report's caution is a deliberate reaction, not institutional timidity

FTC/FDA rules generally require evidence before health claims. COVID produced plenty of counter-examples where narrative outran evidence — ionizers, unproven "UV wands," hydroxychloroquine hype — and did real damage to public trust in the category. That history is almost certainly why this report over-hedges rather than under-hedges. Better marketing of good science is a genuinely useful idea in the abstract; it's a dangerous one to get backwards in this specific field.

6.1 vs. 6.2 standards overlap — mostly not covered, despite how it reads

6.1's list (ASHRAE 241, UL 8802/8803, IEC 62471, ISO 15858, RP 27.1) contains no ozone-testing methodology built for far-UVC's specific spectral profile — that gap in 6.2 is genuine new work, not a restatement. The device-labeling standard in 6.2 overlaps more, since IEC 62471 and RP 27.1 already govern photobiological safety disclosure, but neither mandates the consumer-facing lifetime/power/spectrum labeling 6.2 asks for.

"Don't these markets exist already after COVID?" — partially, but not for far-UVC specifically

Conventional GUV markets in water treatment, surface disinfection, and food processing are genuinely mature. COVID did produce a UV-branded consumer boom, but a lot of it was unregulated, non-far-UVC "UV-C wand" marketing that regulators and researchers have since had to walk back. Far-UVC's specific niche — safe-by-design, continuous, occupied-space use — doesn't yet have that market penetration, which is why the report labels these uses "proposed" rather than evaluated.

Cost-benefit studies as a marketing opportunity — already their playbook, just not narrated that way

The PPE program's engineering studies with Amodo Design directly became the evidence base behind the appropriations wins. Framing future far-UVC cost-benefit work the same way — evidence generation that doubles as the policy and funding pitch — isn't a new idea for them, it's a continuation of what's already working.

Short-term vs. long-term deployment — the logic exists, just isn't named as a doctrine

Recommendation 10 already makes close to this point: requiring long-term safety data before deploying an efficacious, life-saving intervention would be "an excessive application of the precautionary principle." That's the crisis-deployment override, stated but not formalized into a framework. Proposing an explicit two-tier structure — looser evidence standards for emergency deployment, a higher bar for routine or steady-state use — is a genuinely useful structuring idea to bring to the conversation, not something you'd be repeating back to them.

Hatch built small enclosed physical environments for public occupancy. Yahoo/RYOT ran occupied studio production environments specifically during the pandemic, under exactly the kind of built-environment safety constraints this program studies. Cassette operates under active CA HCD regulatory compliance for occupied structures. None of that needs reframing or stretching to fit — it's direct, documented, operational experience with the built-environment side of what Blueprint studies. It doesn't require the AI-risk bridge to justify the conversation, and it's worth leading with on the call, not holding in reserve behind the AI framing.

10 — Your own positioning

Framing yourself as a people manager

This is the center of the case for you, per the recruiter's confirmation that Jake and Siobhan are explicitly sourcing outside the field for people-management strength. Organized in two layers: the abstract philosophy first, then the real, specific evidence and stories underneath it. The philosophy is only worth stating because the examples back it up — lead with the examples in conversation, and let the philosophy surface as the throughline, not the headline.

Leadership philosophy — the abstract version

Stated plainly here so you have the language ready. In the room, these should come out as the explanation for why you did something, not as a list you recite.

  • Structure will set you free. Clear roles, lanes, and process aren't bureaucracy for its own sake — they're what lets people move fast and stay confident without constantly checking whether they're allowed to act.
  • A leader's job is not to know everything or do everyone's job. It's to enable the experts to do theirs, and to orchestrate and unblock the collaboration between them. You're not the smartest person on any of your teams about the domain — you never have been, going back to managing sound engineers and producers who out-craft you technically.
  • When everything is a priority, nothing is a priority. A leader's specific job is to tease that out for people — to make the trade-off explicit instead of letting a team quietly absorb an impossible load.
  • Treat the human and the employee — no dispassion. Relationships are personal even though work is not; that means real openness balanced with real boundaries, not a purely transactional read on people.
  • Be direct and transparent, and be proactive about contact. Have answers. If you don't have them, say so, and go get them — don't perform certainty you don't have.
  • When conflict arises, take the high road. Acknowledge it, don't suppress it, but don't get pulled into the drama — it costs more time and trust than almost any other failure mode.
  • Protect the maker's schedule. Technical and creative specialists need long, uninterrupted blocks to do real work; a manager's job includes shielding that time, not filling it with meetings because your own calendar has the room.
  • Watch-team backup. Build a norm where anyone on the team can flag something that looks off — including junior people flagging something to someone more senior — without it reading as an accusation. The norm only works if being corrected is treated as helpful, every time, out loud.
  • The cost of a bad hire almost always exceeds the cost of delay — but not indefinitely. Speed pressure during rapid scaling is real, but a wrong hire costs more in cleanup, team trust, and re-hiring than the weeks it would have taken to find the right one. The balance point isn't fixed: it shifts with the urgency and the scope/seniority of the role. A short-term contractor gap tolerates more speed than a director-level hire who'll shape the org for years.

Leadership style — the examples

Pillar 3 — Managing experts who are technical first, managers second

Coaching someone into becoming a better manager, not just managing them

Operating rhythm — the actual mechanics

PPPA and a two-tier goal cadence

1:1s run biweekly with individual directs, 45 minutes with a 15-minute buffer if needed, scaling to weekly for a smaller or more senior group and stretching out for larger, less direct spans. Each 1:1 follows a PPPA structure: Progress (open action items from last time, reprioritized against whatever's shifted in the two weeks since), Problems (new issues, and what teams or resources are needed to move them), Priorities (what matters in the current two-week window versus what's slower-churn and can align to longer projects), and Actions (assigned by problem, to a specific individual, even when the work touches a whole team). Goal-setting runs on a parallel cadence at the org level: 3-year, 1-year, and sub-1-year horizons, with annual OKRs cascading into quarterly OKRs. This used to run through a tracker you built yourself; whatever PM tool Blueprint already uses can carry the same structure without you needing to import your old system wholesale.

Holding a high bar without cutting off autonomy

Roles, lanes, and inter-team friction at Yahoo Studios

Across the broader Studios org, people were anxious about being left out of meetings and decisions that touched their work — this wasn't one incident, it was a trend you noticed and got ahead of rather than waiting for it to boil over. The real cause wasn't access, it was unclear roles: without defined lanes, people defaulted to wanting to be in everything just to feel safe. You fixed the root cause by establishing clear roles and lanes of collaboration, and supplemented that with your own communication to actively bridge gaps between teams rather than leaving people to infer context. Result, in plain terms: people stopped complaining about being left out and were able to focus on their own work. The tension worth naming honestly if asked directly: giving people a defined lane necessarily means telling some people they don't own every decision that touches them — that's a real trade-off, not a free lunch, and the payoff was people trusting the structure enough to stop needing to be everywhere.

Speed versus quality in hiring — a live tension at Blueprint right now

How you actually think about sourcing under scaling pressure

Blueprint's own growth — 4 people to 16–21 in under two years — is exactly the condition where hiring pressure and hiring quality pull against each other, and it's a tension you've built real judgment on, not just an opinion about. Your working view: a bad hire almost always costs more than the delay it would have taken to avoid it — in cleanup time, team trust, and the cost of re-running the search — but that's not an absolute rule, it's a balance that shifts with the urgency and the seniority/scope of the role. You think about candidate sourcing across five channels, each with real trade-offs: personal and professional networks (fast, high-trust, direct firsthand read on fit — but requires staying strictly professional to avoid any read of nepotism); extended networks (can target a specific domain or demographic pool, but unproven and unvetted relative to a direct reference); competitors (built-in role, comp, and skill parity — but not every competitor's bar is the same, and a strong resume doesn't guarantee a stable hire); external specialty recruiters (fast and effective for a well-scoped role, but expensive and requires real partner management to avoid mismatched incentives); and blind solicitation (wide reach, lowest signal — sometimes it works, but you're not relying on it for anything senior or urgent). For a role like Managing Director itself — high scope, high seniority, hard to reverse if wrong — the right posture is patience and a narrower, higher-trust channel mix, even under real pressure to fill the seat. This isn't abstract for you: you've written hundreds of job descriptions, run hiring pipelines through LinkedIn and Indeed, and built your own Airtable-based hiring process system for internal use rather than relying entirely on off-the-shelf ATS tools — the kind of operational infrastructure most people managers never bother building for themselves.
